WRITING

Stories that ask difficult questions.

Richard Oswestry writes stories about people, choices and consequences.

Not every story is comfortable.

Some are intended to entertain. Others are intended to make the reader stop and think. Occasionally, a story may challenge what the reader believes, or leave them uncomfortable with the questions it raises.

That is part of the reason for writing it.

The Moral Dilemma

The Moral Dilemma is a controversial story.

Some readers will like it. Some will not.

That is entirely their right.

The story deals with circumstances and choices that are difficult to confront. They are not presented because they are easy, nor because they should be approved.

They are presented because they happened.

A writer has the right to tell a story that he believes must be told.

Telling a story does not mean condoning what happens within it.

Sometimes the purpose of fiction is not to give the reader an answer, but to put them in a position where they must decide what they believe.

The Moral Dilemma is one of those stories.

What Comes Next

The writing continues.

Some stories have been waiting for years. Others arrive unexpectedly. Some begin with a question; others begin with a character, an object, or a moment that refuses to disappear.

The common thread is simple:

What happens next?

And perhaps, more importantly:

What does it mean that we chose it?
The story is told.
The judgement belongs to the reader.
